Method

How the plans are built

A training plan makes claims: that these hours get you to that level, that this drill fixes that weakness, that you will know when you have got there. You should be able to inspect those claims. This page sets them out.

01

What the planner does

You supply four numbers: a horizon in weeks, weekly practice hours, sessions per week, and your honest starting level. The planner multiplies the first two into a total hour budget, credits an allowance for prior experience, and compares the result against four thresholds held for each skill: functional, working competence, professional, and elite.

Then it tells you which one your hours reach. If that is lower than you were hoping, it says so before you start and does the arithmetic on your three options: give it longer, go harder, or want less. That is most of the value here. A plan promising mastery in four weeks is not ambitious, it is just wrong, and it falls apart in week five.

When the hours will not cover a subject properly, whole pillars get cut instead of every pillar getting thinner. Eighty hours across five areas leaves you vaguely acquainted with five things. Eighty hours on two leaves you able to do two things. The plan lists what it left out so you can disagree with the choice.

02

Where the hour estimates come from

They are approximations and should be read as approximations. They are anchored on published work about skill acquisition, on how professional training and apprenticeship in each field is normally structured, and on how long it visibly takes a motivated adult to get to the point where someone will pay them.

They are not measurements of you. Variation between people is large, related experience moves the numbers a lot, and the quality of feedback you can get probably matters more than the raw hour count. Use the thresholds to check your ambition against arithmetic. They are not a schedule your biology has agreed to.

The "10,000 hours" figure gets quoted constantly and misreads the research it came from. That work described how much practice elite violinists had accumulated, with expert coaching throughout. It was never a universal requirement. Time to competence varies enormously between fields, and in most of them the first genuinely useful level arrives far earlier than that number suggests.
03

The training principles

Practice with a target

Practice that improves you is effortful, aimed at something specific you cannot do yet, and followed by some kind of feedback. Repeating what you can already do is maintenance. Every session here names what it is aiming at.

Recall beats rereading

Pulling something out of memory makes it stick. Rereading mostly produces the feeling that it has stuck. Every session opens by writing down what you remember with your notes shut, which takes five minutes and is the cheapest gain available here.

Spacing and mixing

Sessions of the same kind are separated rather than stacked together. Mixed practice feels worse while you are doing it and works better afterwards, which is one of the more reliable and more annoying findings in the research.

Making, not consuming

The share of your week spent taking things in falls as the plan goes on, and the share spent making something rises. Consuming material about a skill is the most comfortable substitute for practising it, and it is how most plans quietly fail.

Gates, not calendars

Each phase ends with criteria someone else could check. Moving on because the weeks have passed is how people arrive at the end of a course with a certificate and nothing they can actually do.

Progress you can see

Streaks are counted in weeks rather than days, because the plans schedule four or five sessions a week and a day streak would break by design. Missing once says one miss is noise. Missing for a fortnight says restarting costs one session rather than the whole plan.

Planned lighter weeks

Anything over ten weeks includes reduced weeks. Rest taken on purpose, before you need it, is what prevents the quiet abandonment that usually turns up around week seven.

04

What this cannot do

It cannot guarantee you will be successful. Skill is one input among several, alongside circumstance, timing, money, health, whatever obligations you already have, and luck. Anyone selling you a straight line from a training plan to a particular life is selling the part they cannot deliver.

It cannot give you feedback. That is the biggest gap between a plan and a coach, and it is why every skill here names the places real signal comes from. A plan run entirely in private will plateau, and you will probably not notice when it does.

It cannot make you do the work. It does track it, though, because coming back to a page that shows nothing changed is a good reason never to come back. The plan counts hours you have banked against the threshold it quoted you at the start, how many weeks running you have kept it up, and which gate criteria you have passed. Those are measurements of work you did, not points awarded for opening the page. There are no celebration animations and nothing gets unlocked, because that trains enthusiasm for an interface rather than for a skill.

Finally, it is a general tool. It knows nothing about your circumstances, your existing commitments or your health. Anything here touching money or physical training is general education rather than professional advice. For decisions that turn on your particular situation, talk to someone qualified who can see it.

05

Data and privacy

There is no account, no server and no analytics. Your plan, your progress, your logs and your reading preferences sit in this browser's local storage and go nowhere else. Clearing your browser data deletes them, and opening the site on another device shows you nothing.

That is a deliberate choice rather than an unfinished feature, and it means the export buttons matter. Download the Markdown or the calendar file if the plan is something you want to keep.
